# Kandela Kulture: Strategic Manifesto

*Same people. Different light. Final draft 1.0 · Nairobi*

## 1. What we believe

We create a context for continuity, so that first impressions can mature into lasting bonds. We do that by offering a different way for people to meet, one that builds on the first encounter and lets more layers be seen. After the sun sets, we keep the light on by letting you pass your flame to others.

We are Kandela Kulture.

The rest of this document explains how that belief becomes a business, a method and a movement, and what we will and will not do on the way.

## 2. The problem we exist to solve

Most people meet each other in only one setting. Colleagues know each other from meetings. Teammates know each other from the pitch. Neighbours know each other from the gate. A relationship that lives in one room is fragile: when the job, the team or the street changes, it ends.

A bond gets stronger when the same people meet in more than one setting. Each new setting shows a different side of a person and gives the relationship another place to live. We call each setting a **vector of contact**. Our work is to add vectors between people who already know each other a little, so that acquaintance can become something that lasts.

The first vector can be anything. It does not have to be dance, and it does not have to be ours. A work team, a campus club, a congregation, a school parents' group and a WhatsApp group are all places we can begin.

## 3. What we are, and what we are not

We are a **fun infrastructure** company. We design and install the conditions in which groups can meet in new ways, learn something that stays with them, and carry the practice on without us.

We are not an events company. An event sells three hours and ends when people walk out. We use an event only as the first spark. The product is what remains after it: a skill, a habit, a toolkit and a connection that keeps going.

We are not a team-building agency and we are not a networking club. Both sell a moment. We sell continuity, and we are willing to be measured on it.

### Team building as a way of life, not a yearly retreat

We treat team building as a rhythm a team lives in, not a single day it attends. The annual retreat is a single day, easy to forget and tied to the calendar, so it fades once people are back at their desks. A rhythm of small, repeated moments is easier to join, fits the working week and becomes part of how the team spends its time.

**What a rhythm looks like (illustrative):**

- **A Spark session** to open: one half-day of play and a first side-by-side task.
- **A monthly hour** that sits inside the working week: a walk, a build, a shared repair or a short game.
- **A quarterly skill evening**, such as first aid or de-escalation with a partner trainer, so the team keeps learning one thing together.
- **A service morning** a few times a year, in a place the team has chosen.
- **A team**, where the team uses our toolkit to host one itself, and members take turns as host.

**Why this angle.** The research we found supports meeting in more than one setting and cooperating side by side (see Appendix A). We found no study that compares an annual retreat with frequent small sessions, so the claim that rhythm beats a retreat is a design bet that we will test and publish, not a fact we will quote.

**How it shapes the business.** Teams join for a year at a time, for the rhythm and not a single event. A team that hosts its own sessions is a team that has taken the flame, which is the outcome we want. We measure it by whether the sessions continue when we step back. We position on lasting value, never on being the cheaper option.

### A reprieve that keeps you moving forward

Every opportunity we offer is tied to what the person or organisation is already trying to achieve. It is a break from the daily routine that still points the same way as your goals. It is not a detour, a distraction or a prize for finishing the real work.

Most team-building and social activity is incidental: pleasant, but unrelated to anything people care about, so it competes with their goals. Ours is meant to serve them.

**How we keep it relevant (illustrative):**

- **A hospital team** wants staff who are calm and ready in an emergency. The context is first aid and peer emotional support with a partner trainer.
- **A company** wants stronger cross-team trust and lower burnout. The context is a shared build or a service morning where teams cover for each other.
- **A student group** wants to grow its volunteer base. The context is a resilience lab that ends with sign-up to a partner's register.
- **A person** wants a new skill and some new friends. The context is a small group learning that skill side by side.

**Our test.** Would this still be worth doing if the social side were taken away? If the skill, the service or the result would not stand on its own, we leave it out. The connection comes with the work. It is not a substitute for it.

**Our first question.** Before we design anything, we ask one thing: what are you already trying to get done this year? The answer decides the context. We then track progress on that goal, so the reprieve can be shown to have moved it forward.

## 4. The method: four moves

**Spark.** People arrive guarded by titles, status and shyness. We open with play: a task, puzzle or challenge that needs real cooperation and little talk. Fun is a prerequisite here, not decoration. It lowers defences, so people can learn and interact honestly.

**Pivot.** We move people from face-to-face to side-by-side. Facing each other can feel like being examined. Working next to each other on a shared task is easier, and it is where talk tends to open up.

**Layer.** Once people are at ease, we add a second and third context: a skill, a service, a making task, a walk. Each layer shows another side of the people involved.

**Pass.** Every session ends with a toolkit that lets participants run the same experience themselves, in their own team, family or street. That is the flame passed on.

**How we facilitate.** Our hosts are architects of context, not people with a microphone. They set the rules, supply the prompts, then step back. The group organises itself, argues, decides and discovers. If the room is mostly listening to us, the session has failed.

## 5. The contexts we open

We begin with contexts that need no equipment, so we can start now and build a track record that attracts partners. Over time, we widen the range.

- **Learn together:** first aid, de-escalation, peer emotional first aid, financial basics, digital basics.
- **Serve together:** school feeding programmes, soup kitchens, hospital wards, prison and rehabilitation programmes, clean-ups.
- **Make together:** carpentry and upcycling, gardening and composting, cooking and preserving, art and music.
- **Move together:** walking, dance, games, sport, day trips.
- **Be quiet together:** reading rooms, silent walks, shared meals where nobody performs.

The rule for any context is simple: people do something real side by side, they enjoy it, and they leave able to do something they could not do before.

## 6. What we offer

**Foundation (for any group).** A facilitated session built on Spark, Pivot, Layer and Pass, plus a Continuity Toolkit the group keeps. We start here, with no specialist equipment.

**Sector tracks (curated for an industry).** We adapt the foundation to a sector's real goals, and every session has to serve one:

- *Companies:* the goal is stronger cross-team trust, steadier teams and readiness under pressure. We offer crisis-response simulations and cross-team challenges that finish with a certified skill, so the time spent counts toward that goal.
- *Colleges and schools:* the goal is a larger, better-trained pool of engaged student volunteers. We offer game-style resilience labs that finish with certified first-aid training and a volunteer sign-up, so the time spent builds that pool.
- *NGOs and civil society:* the goal is durable partnerships and less fatigue. We offer structured peer-storytelling and problem-mapping sessions that leave a working alliance, so the time spent moves the shared work forward.

**Civic service days.** Groups of employees or students volunteer together in places such as schools, kitchens, wards and rehabilitation programmes, then continue with a recurring service circle of their own.

**What clients actually do.** They play, then they work side by side on something real, then they take the lead on how the session unfolds. They do not sit through a talk. They leave with a skill, a small group that already knows each other better, and a kit to repeat it.

## 7. Flagship partnership: first aid for everyone

Our first partnership goal is to make first-aid skills mainstream, working with the Kenya Red Cross Society and St John Ambulance Kenya.

**What each side brings.**

- *The partners:* clinical authority, curriculum, trainers and certification. Kandela does not certify anyone, ever.
- *Kandela:* a culture that makes people want to turn up, a facilitation method that keeps them engaged, and a continuity toolkit that turns each trained person into a source of more trained people.

**The college reserve.** Campus chapters often struggle to keep students engaged. We run a Resilience Lab on campus: a team challenge that moves into hands-on basic life support and triage with certified trainers, and ends with sign-up to the partner's volunteer register. The aim is a growing reserve of young people who are trained, connected and willing.

**The corporate loop.** Companies pay commercial rates for sector-curated sessions. Part of that income funds free or low-cost places for students and community groups.

**First step.** A half-day pilot with one campus chapter or one willing company, co-run with a partner trainer, with agreed success measures.

**The bridge person.** This route is best opened with one person who is already trusted on both sides, such as a seasoned emergency-response practitioner, public-health advocate or community mobilizer with a standing relationship with these institutions. We will identify and invite this person before approaching the institutions formally.

## 8. The civic service pillar

We offer service days to people who want to give time but rarely get a structured way to do so, especially those outside the religious networks that run most charity volunteering. Service is open to people of every faith and none. It is framed around shared humanity and usefulness, not belief.

Service has to be earned and done with dignity.

- We go only where we are invited, in the way the host organisation wants, and under its supervision.
- Photography is off by default. We do not turn the people we serve into content.
- Volunteers are briefed beforehand, vetted where the setting requires it, and never left alone with children, patients or inmates.
- Prison, hospital and school programmes need written approval from the relevant authority before any group enters.
- We ask what the host actually needs, and we are prepared to do the dull, useful job.

## 9. What the evidence supports, and what we will not claim

The ideas under our method are well established: that people who share several settings form stronger bonds, that strong peer bonds at work are linked with engagement and retention, that psychological safety helps teams learn, and that contact across divides works best when people cooperate on a shared goal as equals.

We will not quote precise statistics or named studies in client material until each has been checked at source. Some figures from early notes, such as a stated drop in staff turnover or a specific recent field experiment, are not verified, and they stay out of this manifesto until they are. We would rather make a modest claim we can defend than a big one that collapses under questioning.

Instead of leaning on other people's research, we will build our own proof: pre- and post-session measures of trust and connection, and follow-up checks three and six months later.

## 10. Why this is not already everywhere

Companies already run volunteering days and team-building days. What they rarely do is layer contexts, hand over the means to continue, or measure what survives. Events are easy to buy and report. Continuity is harder to design and slower to show. We choose the harder, slower route on purpose.

## 11. How we earn, and how we protect the mission

**We charge for continuity, not for the room.** Revenue comes from sector tracks, toolkits, follow-up cohorts and train-the-trainer packages, not from ticket sales to a single night.

**Corporate work funds community work.** Commercial clients pay full rates, and that supports places for students and community groups.

**Open and proprietary.** Community rituals and peer circles meant to be copied are open and free. Our corporate curriculum, facilitation frameworks and toolkits are proprietary, and our name, marks and programme titles are to be registered.

## 12. Guardrails

- **Consent and liability.** A participant agreement for every physical or skill-based session, covering risks, media permissions and behaviour. Public-liability cover for every activation.
- **Partner-led skills.** Anything medical, legal or high-risk is taught only by accredited partner facilitators.
- **Written agreements.** A signed memorandum of understanding with each partner covering roles, brand use, revenue, liability and data.
- **Data protection.** Personal data handled in line with Kenya's Data Protection Act.
- **Trademark.** Registration of the name, marks and key programme titles with the Kenya Industrial Property Institute.
- **Safeguarding.** A written safeguarding policy, trained facilitators, and a clear way to raise concerns.
- **Legal review.** Waivers, MoUs and contracts reviewed by a Kenyan lawyer before use. This document is not legal advice.

## 13. How we will know it is working

- **Continuity rate:** the share of groups that run a follow-up session without us.
- **Layering:** the share of participants who meet in two or more settings within six months.
- **Skills:** the number of people certified through partners, and the number who then train others.
- **Reserve:** students and volunteers registered with partner organisations through us.
- **Trust:** change in measured trust and connection after three and six months.
- **Return:** repeat clients and referrals.

## 14. Sequence

**First 3 months.** Run foundation sessions with no equipment. Identify the bridge person. Write the participant agreement and safeguarding policy. Document the method.

**Months 3 to 9.** Run the first-aid pilot with one campus chapter or company. Open two or three service-day relationships. Start measuring continuity.

**Months 9 to 18.** Formalise MoUs. Launch sector tracks. Register marks. Train the first group of facilitators outside the founding team.

## 15. In short

We make it easier for people to know each other in more than one way. We make it fun, because fun makes people open. We make it useful, because skills outlast the evening. And we make it theirs, so the light keeps moving after we leave.

After the sun sets, we keep the light on. Pass it on.

## Appendix A. The research thesis

The research supports a careful claim: people who meet in more than one cooperative setting build more trust, and the group's own norms decide what that trust is used for. It does not support promises about preventing conflict or keeping marriages together, so we do not make them.

### The thesis

**The Shared-Norm Overlap Thesis.** A second context between the same people strengthens their relationship when four conditions hold:

1. The same people meet across settings (overlap).
2. The group has clear, positive norms for how members treat each other.
3. The activity is cooperative and between equals.
4. The load stays manageable, so the extra tie does not exhaust people.

Without the second condition, a shared group can carry harmful norms. Without the fourth, it can wear people out. Kandela's work is to design all four.

### What the evidence supports

| Claim | Evidence | Strength | Caveat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Colleagues who also share another setting trust each other more and perform better | Methot, LePine, Podsakoff and Christian (2016, *Personnel Psychology*): two studies, 168 insurance employees and 182 restaurant and retail staff. Multiplex friendships were tied to higher supervisor ratings, through trust. | Moderate | Correlational. The same study found emotional exhaustion and difficulty maintaining the ties. |
| Cooperative contact between groups reduces prejudice | Pettigrew and Tropp (2006, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*): 713 samples from 515 studies. Equal status, common goals and cooperation strengthen the effect but are not strictly essential. | Strong overall, uneven in detail | A 2026 working paper by Clochard (Osaka University) notes that few of those studies were experiments and that results vary widely. |
| Added closeness at work can also raise conflict | Fasbender and colleagues (2023): 451 and 499 employees in two time-lagged studies. Workplace friendships were linked to more incivility toward coworkers, through role conflict and depleted resources. Journal to be confirmed. | Moderate | Shows the risk, not that friendship is bad. Managing the load matters. |
| A group's norms shape its members' behaviour | McDermott, Fowler and Christakis (2013, *Social Forces*): in Framingham data, divorce spread between friends in clusters two steps out. Birnbaum, Zholtack and Ayal (2022, *Archives of Sexual Behavior*): online exposure to others' infidelity lowered commitment. | Moderate | Works both ways, so norms must be chosen on purpose. The second study used lab-style exposure, not real friend groups. |
| Shared networks go with steadier marriages | Kearns and Leonard (2004, *Journal of Family Psychology*): 347 couples followed from first marriage. Cornwell (2012, *Journal of Marriage and Family*) and Sauter and colleagues (2022, *Personal Relationships*) fit the pattern. | Weak to moderate; illustrative only | The link ran both ways in the first study, and Sauter found the effect for women but not for men. We use it as an example, never as proof. |

### What we say, and what we do not

**We say:** Meeting in more than one setting builds trust. Shared, cooperative activities make it easier. A group's norms matter, so we set them with the group.

**We do not say:** That we prevent backstabbing, keep marriages together or hold people accountable to each other. We found no strong direct evidence for those claims. "Accountability" implies policing, and our evidence supports shared norms and belonging, not surveillance.

### What this means for how we work

1. **Write a short norms charter for every group.** It covers how members treat each other, how they treat each other's commitments, and what the group does not do, such as gossip or pressure.
2. **Design for cooperation between equals.** Use side-by-side tasks where nobody outranks anybody inside the activity.
3. **Cap the load.** Limit how often and how much we ask of members, so a second context adds support without adding strain.
4. **Match the setting to the group.** Offer workplaces a second setting that is genuinely different from work, so people see each other another way.
5. **Measure our own outcomes.** Record trust, conflict and retention before and after, and again at three and six months, so we can build our own evidence.

### Limits of this evidence

Most of these studies come from the United States and Switzerland, and we found none from Kenya. Many are correlational, so they show patterns, not causes. Check every citation at source before it appears in client or investor material.
