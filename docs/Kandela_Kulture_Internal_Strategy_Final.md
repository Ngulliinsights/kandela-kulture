*Final, version 3.0 · Nairobi · 17 November 2026 · Internal: not for client, partner or investor circulation. This replaces the Strategic Manifesto (versions 1.0 and 1.1).*

## 1. The bet in one page

Most groups know each other in only one setting, so their bonds are fragile. Kandela Kulture adds the missing setting in a form people enjoy, and leaves the group with a skill, a ritual and a toolkit so the connection carries on without us. Our tagline is **Same people. Different light.**

We begin with dance communities, where trust already runs deep and almost none of it leaves the floor. Our first proof is a pilot with the Kenya Red Cross Society: first-aid training taken to students through cross-college salsa tours, leaving behind campus Hearths that keep the training and the volunteers going. If it works, it becomes the template for companies, schools, NGOs and neighbourhoods.

Three things make this a business and not a series of events:

1. **We sell continuity, not the room.** Revenue comes from programmes, toolkits, host training and follow-up cohorts.
2. **Every session serves a goal the group already has.** That is why partners and institutions say yes.
3. **We publish what we learn, including what fails.** Credibility is our only moat that cannot be copied.

## 2. The problem

Most people meet each other in only one setting. Colleagues know each other from meetings, teammates from the pitch, neighbours from the gate, dancers from the floor. A relationship that lives in one room ends when the job, the team or the street changes.

A bond gets stronger when the same people meet in more than one setting. Each setting shows another side of a person and gives the relationship another place to live. In our public language, people know each other **face to face** or **side by side**, and most groups only get one. We supply the other, and what the group then does together becomes a **third context** that belongs to them. Internally we call each setting a **vector of contact**: our work is to add vectors between people who already know each other a little.

The first vector can be anything. A work team, a campus club, a congregation, a parents' group and a WhatsApp group are all places we can begin.

## 3. What we are, and what we are not

We are a **fun infrastructure** company: we design and install the conditions in which groups meet in new ways, learn something that stays, and carry the practice on without us.

We are not an events company, a team-building agency or a networking club. Those sell a moment. We sell continuity and are willing to be measured on it.

### A rhythm, not a yearly retreat

An annual retreat is one day, easy to forget and tied to the calendar. We treat team building as a rhythm a team lives in, made of small, repeated moments that fit the working week.

**What a rhythm looks like (illustrative):**

- **A Spark session** to open: a half-day of play and a first side-by-side task.
- **A monthly hour** inside the working week: a walk, a build, a shared repair or a short game.
- **A quarterly skill evening**, such as first aid or de-escalation with a partner trainer.
- **A service morning** a few times a year, in a place the team has chosen.
- **A team-hosted session**, where the team uses our toolkit to run one itself and members take turns as host.

**Evidence status.** We found no study comparing an annual retreat with frequent small sessions. That a rhythm beats a retreat is a **design bet we will test and publish**, not a fact we quote.

**Business consequence.** Teams join for a year at a time. A team that hosts its own sessions has taken the flame, and we measure that by whether sessions continue when we step back. We position on lasting value, never on being the cheaper option.

### Goal first: rest that still moves you

Everything we offer is tied to something the person or organisation is already trying to achieve. It is a break from routine that points the same way as their goals, not a detour or a prize for finishing the real work.

- **Our first question:** what are you already trying to get done this year? The answer decides the context, and we track progress on that goal.
- **Our test:** would this still be worth doing if the social side were taken away? If the skill, the service or the result would not stand alone, we leave it out.

| Who | Their goal | The context we open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A hospital team | Staff calm and ready in an emergency | First aid and peer emotional support with a partner trainer |
| A company | Cross-team trust, lower burnout | A shared build or a service morning where teams cover for each other |
| A student group | A larger volunteer base | A resilience lab that ends with sign-up to a partner's register |
| A person | A new skill and new friends | A small group learning that skill side by side |

## 4. The method

Every session moves through four moves. Members feel an arc (enter through play, do side by side, talk face to face, take it with you); hosts know the moves.

| Move | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **Spark** | People arrive guarded by titles, status and shyness. We open with play: a task, puzzle or game that needs cooperation and little talk. Fun is a prerequisite, not decoration |
| **Pivot** | We move people between face to face and side by side, whichever they were missing. Working next to each other is easier than being examined across a table, and talk opens up |
| **Layer** | We add a second and third context: a skill, a service, a making task, a walk. Each shows another side of the people involved |
| **Pass** | Every session ends with a toolkit so the group can run it without us. That is the flame passed on |

**How we facilitate.** Our hosts (Keepers) are architects of context, not people with a microphone. They set the frame, supply the prompts, then step back. If the room is mostly listening to us, the session has failed.

**Entry options.** Spark must never depend on one activity. Dance is our first door, not a requirement: every format needs a non-dance Spark (a partner game, a team puzzle, a cooking challenge) so that nobody is excluded by body, belief or confidence.

## 5. The contexts we open

We begin with contexts that need no equipment, so we can start now and build a track record that attracts partners.

- **Learn together:** first aid, de-escalation, peer emotional first aid, financial basics, digital basics.
- **Serve together:** school feeding programmes, soup kitchens, hospital wards, prison and rehabilitation programmes, clean-ups.
- **Make together:** carpentry and upcycling, gardening and composting, cooking and preserving, art and music.
- **Move together:** walking, dance, games, sport, day trips.
- **Be quiet together:** reading rooms, silent walks, shared meals where nobody performs.

The rule for any context: people do something real side by side, they enjoy it, and they leave able to do something they could not do before.

## 6. What we offer

**Foundation (any group).** A facilitated session built on the four moves, plus a Continuity Toolkit the group keeps. We start here, with no specialist equipment.

**Sector tracks (curated to a real goal).**

- *Companies:* stronger cross-team trust, steadier teams, readiness under pressure. Crisis-response simulations and cross-team challenges that finish with a certified skill.
- *Colleges and schools:* a larger, better-trained pool of engaged student volunteers. Game-style resilience labs ending in certified first-aid training and a volunteer sign-up.
- *NGOs and civil society:* durable partnerships and less fatigue. Peer-storytelling and problem-mapping sessions that leave a working alliance.
- *Dance and creative communities:* trust that never leaves the floor. The Lighting, suppers, build days and volunteering.
- *Spaces and neighbourhoods:* empty shared rooms. A seeded community over three to six months.

**Civic service days.** Groups volunteer together in schools, kitchens, wards and rehabilitation programmes, then continue with a recurring service circle of their own.

**What clients actually do.** They play, work side by side on something real, then take the lead on how the session unfolds. They do not sit through a talk. They leave with a skill, a small group that knows each other better, and a kit to repeat it.

## 7. Flagship: first aid for everyone

**Aim.** Make first-aid skills mainstream among students and communities. We aim to work with the Kenya Red Cross Society and St John Ambulance Kenya. **No partnership or endorsement is claimed anywhere until it is agreed in writing.**

**Division of labour.** The partners bring clinical authority, curriculum, trainers, certification and the volunteer register. Kandela brings the pull (a culture people want to turn up for), the facilitation method and the continuity toolkit. Kandela does not teach or certify first aid, ever.

### 7.1 The Kenya Red Cross Society pilot (proposal drafted 14 November 2026, not yet sent or agreed)

**Concept.** A cross-college salsa tour that takes first-aid training to students and leaves behind a lasting campus Hearth at each stop.

**Why salsa clubs.** They meet regularly, mix students across courses and years, and put on visible events. Their leaders are believed to carry real influence on campus. This is an **assumption to test**: in the first month we map which clubs on each campus actually draw and connect students. Salsa is the first door, not the only one.

**Each stop.**

1. **Spark:** a social salsa night or partner-dance game (with a non-dance alternative available).
2. **Pivot:** students move into small side-by-side teams.
3. **Layer:** partner trainers lead hands-on practice (bleeding control, recovery position, CPR basics) with a certified path for those who want it.
4. **Pass:** each club leaves with a toolkit to run a refresher, and students are invited to sign up to the partner's volunteer register.

**Scale.** Four to six campuses over one semester, to be agreed with the partner and limited by trainer capacity.

**Campus Hearths.** The goal is a recognised club on each campus, formed with institutional approval, with a small first-aid-trained core, a short code of conduct and a regular rhythm. Once it exists it can host refreshers, awareness days, service mornings and any programme the partner already runs. *Naming note:* the Brand Identity Guide calls a local chapter a **Hearth**. The draft proposal says "chapter". Use "campus Hearth (chapter)" at first mention in partner documents and keep the Hearth term internally.

**Wider partners.** Other clubs (sports, drama, hiking, student associations) join as hosts so a Hearth is never tied to one social circle. Faith and student religious groups join on equal terms; the programme stays open to every faith and none. **Estate fellowships** are read as residents' fellowships in housing estates, a second audience for after the campus phase because neighbours are often first on the scene. The proposal asks the partner to correct this reading.

### 7.2 Phasing: gates, not a calendar

Each phase must earn the next, and each new Hearth is lit from one that exists. Only the first phase has a fixed time frame.

| Phase | What happens | Gate to pass |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Ground** (about the first month) | Map clubs, agree the memorandum, run one half-day trial on one campus with a salsa club | Agreed trial results (attendance, completion, sign-ups) and the institution's approval |
| **2. Light** | The tour proper across a few campuses; each stop is co-hosted by student leaders from the stop before | Clubs can run a refresher without us; Hearths are ready to register |
| **3. Root** | Hearths register as recognised clubs; neighbouring clubs and faith or student religious groups join because members invite them; Hearths take on a second programme | Hearths keep going on their own and carry a second programme |
| **4. Spread** | Estate fellowships and other community groups, brought in by members who live there, with safeguarding adapted to each setting and partner trainers leading training | A joint review with the partner |

**Principles.** Growth is pulled, not pushed: a phase widens only where students or members ask. Every phase leaves proof we can show the next institution. The pace follows trainer capacity, and we can slow or stop at any gate.

### 7.3 Pilot economics (a gap the proposal does not yet close)

The draft proposal says what each side does but not who pays for trainers' time, venues, insurance, facilitation, printing the toolkit or transport between campuses. Before sending, we need a one-page costing and a position on who bears each cost. Our model is that **corporate work funds community work**: commercial clients pay full rates for sector-curated sessions, and part of that income underwrites free or low-cost places for students and community groups. Until corporate revenue exists, the pilot needs a stated funding source (founder funds, grant, sponsor or partner contribution). Disclosed sponsors follow the Brand Identity Guide rules.

### 7.4 The bridge person

This route is best opened by one person already trusted on both sides, such as an emergency-response practitioner, public-health advocate or community mobilizer with a standing relationship with the institutions. The proposal leaves the bridge contact as a placeholder. We identify and invite this person before any formal approach, with a written advisory role (scope, term, credit) so the work never depends on one individual.

## 8. The civic service pillar

We offer service days to people who want to give time but rarely get a structured way to do so, especially outside the religious networks that run most charity volunteering. Service is open to every faith and none, framed around shared humanity and usefulness.

Service has to be earned and done with dignity:

- We go only where we are invited, in the way the host wants, under its supervision.
- Photography is off by default. We do not turn the people we serve into content.
- Volunteers are briefed, vetted where the setting requires it, and never left alone with children, patients or inmates.
- Prison, hospital and school programmes need written approval from the relevant authority before any group enters.
- We ask what the host actually needs, and we are prepared to do the dull, useful job.

## 9. Why this is not already everywhere

Companies already run volunteering days and team-building days. What they rarely do is layer contexts, hand over the means to continue, or measure what survives. Events are easy to buy and report. Continuity is harder to design and slower to show. We choose the harder, slower route on purpose, and our defence is the written method, trained hosts and published evidence, not secrecy.

## 10. How we earn, and how we protect the mission

- **We charge for continuity, not the room.** Revenue: sector tracks, Kandela Rhythm memberships, toolkits, host training and playbooks, follow-up cohorts and train-the-trainer packages. Never ticket sales to a single night.
- **Corporate work funds community work.** Full commercial rates support places for students and community groups.
- **Open and proprietary.** Rituals and peer circles meant to be copied are open and free. Our curricula, facilitation frameworks, host training, playbooks and measurement templates are proprietary, as are the name, marks and programme titles.
- **Members are never the product.** No sale or sharing of contacts, ever.

## 11. Guardrails

- **Consent and liability.** A participant and media agreement for every physical or skill-based session, and public-liability insurance for every activation. Partner-dance sessions add an explicit touch-and-opt-out norm.
- **Partner-led skills.** Anything medical, legal or high-risk is taught only by accredited partner facilitators. Accreditation does not remove our own duty of care.
- **Written agreements.** A signed MoU with each partner covering roles, brand use, revenue, liability and data. No use of any partner's name or emblem without written approval.
- **Safeguarding.** A written policy, a named lead, trained facilitators and a clear reporting route. Sessions are 18+ by default. Campus sessions need an age check or institution-approved consent for any student under 18, and no one under 18 appears in content.
- **Data protection.** Personal data handled in line with Kenya's Data Protection Act, with consent per purpose.
- **Institutions.** Club registration and events go through each institution's own approval process.
- **Trademark.** Clearance search, then filing with the Kenya Industrial Property Institute (KIPI) for the name, mark, wordmark and key programme titles.
- **Legal review.** Waivers, MoUs and contracts reviewed by a Kenyan lawyer before use. This document is not legal advice.

## 12. Risks and what we do about them

| Risk | What we do |
| --- | --- |
| The salsa-influence assumption is wrong | Map clubs in month one; run the Ground trial before committing to a tour; use other clubs as hosts |
| Dance excludes people (faith, body, confidence) | Non-dance Spark always available; faith groups invited as equals; dancing never required for training or chapter membership |
| Trainer capacity limits scale | Size the tour to capacity; gates, not dates; slow or stop at any gate |
| Dependence on one partner or one bridge person | Written advisory role; second partner (St John Ambulance Kenya) approached after the first conversation; reusable method |
| Institution refuses recognition | Approval is a gate in Ground; plan B is a Hearth open to students across campuses that is not campus-registered |
| Harm during physical or partner-dance activity | Consent norms, insurance, venue checks, accredited trainers only for risky skills |
| Reputational risk from using a partner's name | Written approval before any use; no endorsement claims |
| Pilot cost with no revenue yet | Costing and funding source agreed before the first stop (section 7.3) |
| Overclaiming from thin evidence | Evidence rules in section 14; publish results including failures |

## 13. How we will know it is working

**Pilot measures (to be agreed with the partner before the first stop):** students trained and certified; sign-ups to the volunteer register; Hearths formed and clubs and institutions engaged; students who return for a second session; clubs that run a refresher without us.

**Company-wide measures:**

- **Continuity rate:** the share of groups that run a follow-up session without us.
- **Layering:** the share of participants who meet in two or more settings within six months.
- **Skills:** people certified through partners, and how many then train others.
- **Reserve:** students and volunteers registered with partners through us.
- **Trust:** change in measured trust and connection before, and at three and six months after.
- **Return:** repeat clients, renewals and referrals.
- **Integrity:** zero consent complaints; photo take-downs within 24 hours.

## 14. Evidence rules

The ideas under our method are well established: people who share several settings form stronger bonds, strong peer bonds at work are linked with engagement and retention, psychological safety helps teams learn, and contact across divides works best when people cooperate on a shared goal as equals. Appendix A sets out the thesis and sources.

We will not quote precise statistics or named studies in client, partner or investor material until each has been checked at source. Figures from early notes, such as a stated drop in staff turnover or a specific recent field experiment, are unverified and stay out. We would rather make a modest claim we can defend than a big one that collapses under questioning. Instead of leaning on other people's research, we build our own proof: pre- and post-session measures, with follow-ups at three and six months.

## 15. Sequence

**Readiness (before the first stop).** Write the participant and media agreement, the safeguarding policy and the code of conduct. Name the safeguarding lead. Arrange insurance and a lawyer's review. Run the trademark clearance. Document the method and build the first toolkit. Identify the bridge person. Cost the pilot. Reserve the social handles.

**Then the gates in section 7.2:** Ground, Light, Root, Spread. In parallel and without waiting on the partner, keep running equipment-free foundation sessions with dance communities and open two or three service-day relationships, so that we have our own results to show.

**After the first gates are passed:** formalise MoUs, launch sector tracks for companies and schools, register the marks, and train the first facilitators outside the founding team.

## Appendix A. The research thesis

The research supports a careful claim: people who meet in more than one cooperative setting build more trust, and the group's own norms decide what that trust is used for. It does not support promises about preventing conflict or keeping marriages together, so we do not make them.

### The thesis

**The Shared-Norm Overlap Thesis.** A second context between the same people strengthens their relationship when four conditions hold:

1. The same people meet across settings (overlap).
2. The group has clear, positive norms for how members treat each other.
3. The activity is cooperative and between equals.
4. The load stays manageable, so the extra tie does not exhaust people.

Without the second condition, a shared group can carry harmful norms. Without the fourth, it can wear people out. Our work is to design all four.

### What the evidence supports

| Claim | Evidence | Strength | Caveat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Colleagues who also share another setting trust each other more and perform better | Methot, LePine, Podsakoff and Christian (2016, *Personnel Psychology*): two studies, 168 insurance employees and 182 restaurant and retail staff. Multiplex friendships were tied to higher supervisor ratings, through trust. | Moderate | Correlational. The same study found emotional exhaustion and difficulty maintaining the ties. |
| Cooperative contact between groups reduces prejudice | Pettigrew and Tropp (2006, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*): 713 samples from 515 studies. Equal status, common goals and cooperation strengthen the effect but are not strictly essential. | Strong overall, uneven in detail | A 2026 working paper by Clochard (Osaka University) notes that few of those studies were experiments and that results vary widely. |
| Added closeness at work can also raise conflict | Fasbender and colleagues (2023): 451 and 499 employees in two time-lagged studies. Workplace friendships were linked to more incivility toward coworkers, through role conflict and depleted resources. Journal to be confirmed. | Moderate | Shows the risk, not that friendship is bad. Managing the load matters. |
| A group's norms shape its members' behaviour | McDermott, Fowler and Christakis (2013, *Social Forces*): in Framingham data, divorce spread between friends in clusters two steps out. Birnbaum, Zholtack and Ayal (2022, *Archives of Sexual Behavior*): online exposure to others' infidelity lowered commitment. | Moderate | Works both ways, so norms must be chosen on purpose. The second study used lab-style exposure, not real friend groups. |
| Shared networks go with steadier marriages | Kearns and Leonard (2004, *Journal of Family Psychology*): 347 couples followed from first marriage. Cornwell (2012, *Journal of Marriage and Family*) and Sauter and colleagues (2022, *Personal Relationships*) fit the pattern. | Weak to moderate; illustrative only | The link ran both ways in the first study, and Sauter found the effect for women but not for men. We use it as an example, never as proof. |

### What we say, and what we do not

**We say:** Meeting in more than one setting builds trust. Shared, cooperative activities make it easier. A group's norms matter, so we set them with the group.

**We do not say:** That we prevent backstabbing, keep marriages together or hold people accountable to each other. We found no strong direct evidence for those claims. "Accountability" implies policing; our evidence supports shared norms and belonging, not surveillance.

### What this means for how we work

1. **Write a short norms charter for every group** covering how members treat each other and each other's commitments, and what the group does not do, such as gossip or pressure.
2. **Design for cooperation between equals.** Side-by-side tasks where nobody outranks anybody inside the activity.
3. **Cap the load.** Limit how often and how much we ask of members.
4. **Match the setting to the group.** Offer workplaces a second setting that is genuinely different from work.
5. **Measure our own outcomes.** Trust, conflict and retention before and after, and again at three and six months.

### Limits of this evidence

Most of these studies come from the United States and Switzerland, and we found none from Kenya. Many are correlational, so they show patterns, not causes. Check every citation at source before it appears in client or investor material.
